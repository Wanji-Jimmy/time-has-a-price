TIME HAS A PRICE — QUICK JUDGE ANSWERS
Keep the first answer to one sentence; expand only if asked.

What did you build?
A mathematical decision lab for deciding whether to continue, buy time budget, or stop when waiting has a cost.

Why is this about Time?
Time changes both the available actions and the objective: each market day consumes budget, and unused budget has value at exit.

Why is this interesting if the model stops?
Stopping is a valid action; our overengineered optimizer makes the hidden price of waiting explicit instead of forcing a trade.

What is your contribution?
We combine a purchasable time budget and optimal stopping with a 30-step planner, a controlled EMA comparison, and an inspectable replay and audit.

How does the score work?
It weights logarithms of final cash and remaining budget, so both have diminishing marginal value; alpha is a chosen time preference.
If asked: U = alpha × ln(1+L/90) + (1-alpha) × ln(1+B/100), for positive budget. Exhaustion has utility zero.

What is Bellman planning, in plain English?
Compare each action's average future score, allowing later choices to respond to new information, then choose the highest-valued action now.

Why 30 steps instead of two?
Future opportunities to observe and act can matter; longer planning changes 6 of 192 fixed-state actions, although stable historical improvement is unproven.

Does the planner see future prices?
It uses a frozen empirical distribution for possible futures; the realized replay path is not supplied to it when choosing an action.

What is the strongest concrete result?
At the baseline, both planners preserve 100 cash and 90 budget seconds by exiting; constrained BTC EMA ends with about 100.09 cash and 5 budget seconds.

Is the changed decision better?
The model prefers it in expected utility, but its paired historical interval includes zero, so this replay does not establish an improvement.
If asked: BTC, price 8, alpha .02, L=45; realized utility difference -0.000708, block-30 95% interval [-0.003254, +0.001074].

When does it buy time?
Paid prices 8 and 2 produce no purchases during replay trading; at price 2, alpha .1 and L=20, it exits and exchanges 6 cash for three packs at settlement.
That terminal exchange leaves 94 cash and 65 budget seconds; it does not extend trading. Free time produces 65 mid-trade purchases across 51 replay accounts.

Are the prices real? Is the time real?
Prices are observed Binance daily data; budget seconds, their prices, and utility preferences are transparent model assumptions.

What data did you use?
The market distribution is estimated from BTC in 2022–2025 and frozen; replays use BTC and ETH daily data from January through September 2026.

Was 2026 a blind test?
Those data had already been inspected, so this is historical re-evaluation; genuinely unseen data are the next test.

Are the 61,344 episodes independent?
No; they combine settings and controllers over overlapping windows, so we use paired block-bootstrap intervals and a non-overlapping-start sensitivity check.

Is the EMA comparison fair?
Constrained controllers share costs, budget and terminal exchange rules; the original unrestricted EMA is shown separately as a cash-only reference.

Is the 30-step solution exact?
It covers the full finite horizon, but wealth uses interpolation; it is an approximate solution to a specified finite model, not an exact optimum for real markets.

How did you verify it?
Twelve synthetic tests and a separate audit check balances, costs, budgets, terminal exchanges and summaries, covering 391,772 saved ledger events.

What does the app calculate live?
The interface selects among saved, reproducible experiment results; the Python solver and replay pipeline produced the underlying data.

Did you use open source or AI?
We credit Binance data and the QuantConnect LEAN EMA reference; AI assisted coding, analysis and interface production.

What is the main limitation?
Decisions depend on the assumed market distribution and time preference, and the 2026 replay is not unseen evidence.

What would you do next?
Freeze the current rules, test genuinely unseen data, and check whether different market distributions and utility assumptions change the conclusion.

Can this apply beyond trading?
The same finite-budget question could describe computation, experiments or waiting for opportunities; those applications would need their own data and assumptions.
